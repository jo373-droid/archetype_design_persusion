# Unity: The Principle of Shared Identity

[← Back to Persuasion](README.md)

> **Unity:** Shared identity moves people.

## What is the Unity principle?

Unity is Robert Cialdini's seventh principle of persuasion. It explains how people are more likely to cooperate with, support, or say yes to someone they recognize as part of a shared group or identity.

Unity is different from **Liking**. Liking is based on positive feelings toward another person, often because of similarity, familiarity, or cooperation. Unity is based on a deeper sense of **"we"**: being part of the same family, community, profession, school, team, culture, organization, or other meaningful group.

The key idea is that a persuasive message becomes stronger when it honestly connects the request to an identity the audience already shares with the communicator.

## When and why is Unity used?

Unity is useful when a communicator needs to build cooperation around a shared identity or common purpose. It can be especially effective in:

- **Team communication:** Employees are more likely to support a goal when they see themselves as part of the same team.
- **Community campaigns:** A message can connect people through a shared neighborhood, city, school, or community.
- **Education:** Students can be encouraged by identifying with a shared school or class identity.
- **Marketing:** Brands can build communities around customers who share a lifestyle, interest, or value.
- **Fundraising and nonprofit work:** Appeals can connect supporters to a community or cause they already identify with.
- **Leadership:** Leaders can frame organizational goals as something "we" accomplish together rather than something imposed on "you."

Unity works because a shared identity can make another person's interests feel more connected to our own. Cialdini's research describes this as a sense of "we-ness."

## Practical applications

### 1. Use inclusive language

Words such as **we, us, our, together, community, and team** can make a shared identity more visible.

**Example:**

Instead of:

> "You need to help the department reach its goal."

Try:

> "Together, we can help our department reach its goal."

The second message emphasizes membership in a shared group.

### 2. Connect a request to a real shared identity

A communicator should identify something the audience genuinely shares.

**Example for a college organization:**

> "As students at our university, we can make our campus more welcoming for everyone."

The message connects the action to an existing student identity.

### 3. Build a sense of community around a brand

A brand can invite customers to see themselves as members of a community rather than simply buyers.

**Example:**

> "Join a community of creators who believe better design starts with curiosity."

The message uses a shared identity—creators—to connect the audience to the brand.

### 4. Create a shared team goal

Managers can use Unity when introducing a project or organizational change.

**Example:**

> "We're one team, and this project gives us an opportunity to solve the problem together."

This framing emphasizes a common identity and goal.

### 5. Broaden the identity when possible

Unity should not be used to deliberately create hostility toward outsiders. A stronger ethical approach is to find the **widest truthful group identity** that includes the people involved.

For example, two departments may disagree about a project. Instead of emphasizing "our department" versus "their department," a leader could emphasize:

> "We're all part of the same organization, and we share the same goal of serving our customers."

This creates a broader "we."

## Real-world research example

One important Unity study involved committed Manchester United supporters. Researchers made the participants' football identity salient and then observed whether they helped an injured person wearing a Manchester United shirt, a Liverpool shirt, or a plain sports shirt.

Participants were more likely to help when the injured person appeared to share their group identity. A later experiment showed that changing the identity made salient could also change who was perceived as part of the group.

The research demonstrates an important feature of Unity: **the identity that is active in a situation can influence who people perceive as "one of us."**

## Design recommendations

### Imagery

Choose images that clearly communicate **belonging, collaboration, and shared identity**.

Good choices include:

- Teams working toward the same goal
- Groups participating in a shared activity
- Students collaborating
- Community events
- People wearing or using recognizable group symbols
- Diverse groups connected through a common purpose

Avoid imagery that creates an unnecessary "us versus them" message.

### Colors

Use a consistent color system that makes the page feel cohesive.

Suggested approach:

- **Primary:** a strong, confident color associated with the group's identity
- **Secondary:** a complementary neutral for backgrounds and supporting content
- **Accent:** one contrasting color for calls to action
- Keep contrast high enough for accessibility and readability

For a school or organization, using its established brand colors can reinforce an authentic shared identity.

### Typography

Use typography that feels approachable and organized.

Recommended hierarchy:

- **H1:** bold display type for the page title
- **H2:** strong headings for major sections
- **Body:** highly readable sans-serif or serif text
- **Quotes/callouts:** slightly larger or italicized text to emphasize shared-language examples

Keep the typography consistent with the rest of the project so Unity feels like part of the same reference guide.

### Wording

Effective Unity wording should be:

- Inclusive
- Specific
- Authentic
- Positive
- Focused on a genuine shared identity

Useful phrases include:

- "We can..."
- "Together, we..."
- "As members of..."
- "Our community..."
- "People like us..."
- "We're working toward..."
- "Join our community..."

Do not claim that an audience shares an identity when it does not. The persuasive message should be truthful.

## Ethical use

Unity is powerful because it can strengthen cooperation, but the same mechanism can be misused to divide people.

Use Unity ethically by:

1. Identifying a **genuine** shared identity.
2. Being truthful about group membership.
3. Making the shared identity as inclusive as the situation honestly allows.
4. Avoiding stereotypes or attacks on people outside the group.
5. Never using belonging as a substitute for accurate information.

The goal should be to create a larger sense of **"we,"** not to manufacture an unnecessary **"us versus them."**
